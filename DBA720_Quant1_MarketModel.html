Excel work

  1. For each series compute AVERAGE, VAR.S, STDEV.S, SKEW and KURT. Excel's KURT already returns excess kurtosis, so a normal distribution scores 0, not 3.
  2. Compute COVARIANCE.S and CORREL between the two series.

Excel trap. The Data Analysis ToolPak's Covariance tool returns population covariance (dividing by n), while COVARIANCE.S and VAR.S divide by n−1. Mixing them changes the number. The ratio Cov/Var is unaffected only if you are consistent — use the .S functions throughout.

Interpretation

1. What the kurtosis figure does to everything else

The stock's excess kurtosis is 2.2848. Explain what that means and what it implies for the risk measures built on this data.

Excess kurtosis of 2.2848 means the distribution has fatter tails than the normal — extreme monthly returns occur materially more often than a normal distribution with the same standard deviation would predict. The consequences run through everything downstream. Standard deviation and variance still exist, but they understate the probability of large adverse moves, so any risk measure calibrated on them — Value at Risk most obviously — is too optimistic in the tail. Confidence intervals and t-statistics that assume normality are mis-sized. Sharpe ratios compare assets on mean and standard deviation alone, so they are blind to exactly the tail risk that distinguishes this asset. And option-pricing models resting on lognormal returns will misprice out-of-the-money options. This is why Assignment 1 asked for the QQ plot and the density plot before any theory was taught: the distributional problem is established first, and then the course spends the term applying tools that assume it away. Cryptocurrency returns are considerably worse on this dimension than the equities here.

2. Alpha, honestly

The estimated alpha is 0.4878% per month. What would it take before you would describe that as evidence of manager skill?

Very little, on this evidence. First, statistical significance: with 36 observations and residual variance this size, the standard error on the intercept is large, and the point estimate is almost certainly indistinguishable from zero. Second, model specification: alpha is a residual, so it captures whatever the single market factor omits. Add size, value, or momentum factors and apparent alpha routinely shrinks. Third, net of costs: gross alpha before fees, trading costs and taxes is not a return anyone receives. Fourth, survivorship: if this asset entered the sample because it survived and performed, the estimate is biased upward before it is computed. Bianchi and Babiak (2022) confront all four of these in the crypto-fund setting, and their finding of positive abnormal returns net of expenses is interesting precisely because those corrections are hard to make there — the sample is short, the factor model for digital assets is unsettled, and fund attrition is high.

3. The connection worth naming

Where else in this course have you computed Cov/Var, under a different name?

Three places, and they are the same estimator. Beta here. The minimum-variance hedge ratio in the derivatives module, h = Cov(ΔS,ΔF)/Var(ΔF). And the market-model slope used to generate expected returns in an event study, which is where beta goes to work as a research tool rather than a risk measure. Each is a regression coefficient: the covariance of two series scaled by the variance of the explanatory one. Naming that connection in an essay costs a sentence and demonstrates that the quantitative material is understood as one structure rather than four procedures.
