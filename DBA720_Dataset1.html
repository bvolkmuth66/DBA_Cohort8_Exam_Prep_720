Dataset 1 — Blockchain Platform Adoption

Variable identification, multiple regression in Excel, and whether the predictors can honestly be ranked.

Revised in v3.0. The original data had predictors correlating between 0.71 and 0.91, with variance inflation factors near 9 and an R2 of 0.976. Under collinearity that severe the individual coefficients are unstable, so “strongest predictor” was not a well-defined quantity even though the exercise graded it. The data has been regenerated with separated predictors, and a diagnostic phase has been added.

The situation

A blockchain software company is concerned that many users create accounts but never become long-term active users. Management surveyed 30 customers on four measures, each on a 1–7 scale.

Measure A

The degree to which a user believes the platform is reliable, honest and dependable.

Measure B

The degree to which a user believes their information and digital assets are protected.

Measure C

The degree to which a user finds the platform easy to learn and operate.

Measure D

The likelihood that a user plans to continue using the platform.

Phase 2 — Excel analysis

  1. Compute the mean and standard deviation of each variable.
  2. Compute the correlation of each predictor with Adoption Intention, and the correlations among the three predictors.
  3. Run the multiple regression with Adoption Intention as the dependent variable (Data → Data Analysis → Regression, or LINEST). Record R2, adjusted R2, and the three coefficients.
All three predictors are measured on the same 1–7 scale, so their raw coefficients are directly comparable. Had the scales differed, you would need standardised (beta) coefficients before comparing them at all.

Phase 3 — Can the predictors be ranked?

Before trusting any coefficient comparison, check whether the predictors are separable. Compute the pairwise correlations among the three predictors, then the variance inflation factor for each:

VIF_j = 1 / (1 - R2_j)

where R2_j comes from regressing predictor j on the OTHER predictors.

Rule of thumb: VIF above 5 is a concern, above 10 is serious.


Predictor correlations: Trust↔Security 0.4374, Trust↔Ease 0.054, Security↔Ease 0.0974. VIFs: 1.24, 1.24, 1.01 — all close to 1, comfortably below any threshold of concern. The predictors are near-orthogonal, so each coefficient is estimated on genuinely independent variation and comparing them is defensible.

Why this phase exists. Had the VIFs come back near 9, the correct answer would have been the opposite, and the honest finding would have been that the three constructs cannot be separated in this sample — you would report the joint explanatory power and decline to rank. Recognising when a question cannot be answered from the data is the judgement a comprehensive examination is testing, and it is not something a regression output announces on its own.

Interpretation

1. Recommendation to management

If the company could improve only one area, which should it prioritise, and what caveat would you attach?

Trust, on this evidence — it carries the largest coefficient and the strongest simple correlation with adoption intention. Three caveats belong with that recommendation. First, the model explains 66% of variance, so roughly 34% is driven by factors not measured here — fees, network effects, regulatory news, the behaviour of the wider crypto market. Second, these are cross-sectional perceptions, so the finding is associational: it is equally consistent with users who intend to keep using the platform rationalising higher trust. Third, and most practically, trust is not a lever management can pull directly. It is an outcome of things they can act on — uptime, custody arrangements, incident transparency, support responsiveness. A recommendation to “increase trust” is not actionable; a recommendation to publish audited reserve attestations and cut incident response times is.

2. Intention is not behaviour

The dependent variable measures stated intention to continue using the platform. The company's actual problem is dormant accounts. How much does this analysis really tell them?

Less than it appears. The intention–behaviour gap is well documented in technology-acceptance research, and it is the specific problem Albayati, Kim and Rho (2021) confront: cryptocurrency wallet accounts grew sixfold while actual trading stayed dormant, which is precisely a population reporting positive intentions and not acting on them. Survey respondents are also a self-selected subset — users engaged enough to answer a survey are not the dormant users management is worried about, so the sample may systematically exclude the phenomenon of interest. A stronger design would link survey responses to observed account activity and model actual usage, or at minimum validate that stated intention predicts subsequent behaviour in this population before treating it as a proxy.
