Options in DerivaGem

Driving the tool, reading its output, and checking the result against an arbitrage identity.

Scope note. This module deliberately does not ask you to build Black–Scholes from d1 and d2 in a spreadsheet. The instructor's exam note said students “may want to review” the class derivatives material and the DerivaGem spreadsheet — a pointer to a tool, not an instruction to reconstruct the model. What follows is entering parameters, reading values, and knowing what they mean.

Part A — Black–Scholes in DerivaGem

  1. Open DerivaGem and choose the Equity underlying type.
  2. Select Analytic European as the option type — that is the Black–Scholes engine.
  3. Enter the six parameters above.
  4. Calculate, then read off the call and put values and the Greeks the sheet reports.
DerivaGem reports vega per one percentage point of volatility and theta per calendar day. If your figures are off by a factor of 100 or 365, that convention is usually why.

Enter what DerivaGem reports

Call $3.9052, put $4.6213, Δcall 0.5156, Δput -0.4844, Γ 0.0376, vega 0.1409.

Two things worth reading off these numbers. Δcall − Δput = 1 exactly, which is put–call parity differentiated — a useful arithmetic check that you entered the parameters correctly. And the put is worth more than the call because the strike sits $2.00 above the current price, so the call starts out of the money.

Part B — The same option on a binomial tree

Still in DerivaGem, switch the option type to Binomial European and set the number of time steps to 2. Leave every other parameter unchanged and recalculate.

The tree returns $3.8435 against Black–Scholes $3.9052 — a gap of $0.0617. Two steps is a coarse discretisation of a continuous price process; the tree converges on the analytic value as steps increase. Raise the step count to 50 in the same sheet and watch the difference close. That convergence is the whole reason the binomial model is taught alongside Black–Scholes rather than as a rival to it — and unlike Black–Scholes, the tree extends to American options, where early exercise has to be checked at every node.

Part C — Put–call parity as a check

Do not price the put again. Recover it from the call you already have:

p = c + K*EXP(-r*T) - S

Parity gives $4.6213, identical to the value DerivaGem reported. That is not a coincidence and not a rounding accident — parity is a no-arbitrage identity, not a model. It holds whatever option-pricing model you use, because violating it is a riskless profit: buy the cheap side, sell the dear side, hold to expiry. It is therefore the fastest sanity check on any option figure you produce, including one a spreadsheet handed you.

It is also the same law-of-one-price logic Lyons and Viswanath-Natraj (2023) apply to stablecoin pegs, where the arbitrage is engineered rather than assumed and the paper measures what happens to the peg when the engineering improves.

Interpretation

1. Vega and crypto derivatives

Vega is largest for at-the-money options with time remaining. What does that imply for anyone writing options on a cryptocurrency rather than an equity?

The writer is short vega, so value is destroyed when volatility rises — and crypto volatility is both higher and far less stable than equity volatility. Worse, Black–Scholes assumes constant volatility and lognormal returns, while crypto returns are fat-tailed and volatility-clustered, so realised moves arrive in bursts the model treats as nearly impossible. Practically: implied volatilities move violently, delta-hedging a short position demands rebalancing precisely when liquidity is worst, and the model's price is least trustworthy exactly when the position is most exposed. Nakagawa and Sakemoto (2022) make the related point on the correlation side — co-movement itself shifts with market uncertainty, so a hedge calibrated in calm conditions is not the hedge you hold in a crisis.

2. Model risk

DerivaGem gave you a number twice, by two different methods. Under what circumstances would you distrust both?

Both rest on the same economics: continuous trading, no transaction costs, a known constant volatility, a lognormal price process, and free borrowing and lending at the risk-free rate. The binomial tree is not an independent check on Black–Scholes — it converges to it because it assumes the same world, which is why the agreement in Part B is reassuring about arithmetic and says nothing about realism. Distrust both when trading is discontinuous (gaps, halts, exchange outages), when volatility is stochastic or jumps, when the underlying has fat tails, when funding is unavailable at the assumed rate, or when the position is large enough to move the market it is hedged in. Jorion's (2000) account of LTCM is this failure mode: the models were not obviously wrong, the assumption that positions could be financed and unwound continuously was.
