Full Analysis: Treasury Allocation

A narrative, a dataset, and no instructions about which statistics to run. The closest thing here to an actual examination question.

How this differs from the other modules. Everywhere else the page tells you what to compute. Here it does not. Deciding which analyses the question calls for is the skill being practised, because that is what an open exam prompt demands. Phase 1 is where that judgement happens — work it before scrolling on.

The brief

You sit on the investment committee of a regional bank. The treasury has $40,000,000 to allocate for a three-to-five year horizon under a capital preservation mandate: the committee's stated priority is protecting principal, with return a secondary objective.

Two externally managed funds are under consideration. You have 48 months of returns for both, and for a broad market index over the same period. The risk-free rate is 0.25% per month.

The board has asked for a recommendation and the analysis behind it.

Phase 3 — The recommendation

Write your recommendation for the board

Three or four paragraphs. State the recommendation, give the evidence, and be explicit about what the analysis cannot tell them.


Recommend Fund B. Fund A returns more on average — 1.821% per month against 1.1581% — and that is the only measure on which it wins. Everything else points the other way.

Fund A's standard deviation is 8.2162, roughly 2.9 times Fund B's 2.8263. Adjusting return for that dispersion reverses the ranking: Sharpe ratios are 0.1912 for A against 0.3213 for B. The regressions sharpen it further. Fund A carries a beta of 1.6261, so most of its return is compensation for market exposure the committee could buy more cheaply through an index; its alpha is -0.32 — negative, meaning the manager has destroyed value relative to that exposure. Fund B runs a beta of 0.6457 with a positive alpha of 0.308. On the evidence, A is a leveraged market position and B is a genuinely lower-risk one.

The distributional evidence is decisive under a capital preservation mandate. Fund A's returns are strongly left-skewed (-2.9322) with excess kurtosis of 14.1199 — far from normal, with losses clustered in rare, severe months. Its worst single month was -40.36%, against -6.17% for Fund B. This matters twice over: the mandate is to protect principal, and a single-month drawdown of that size is precisely the outcome the mandate exists to prevent; and the Sharpe ratio itself is unreliable for Fund A, because standard deviation treats upside and downside symmetrically and cannot see a fat left tail. Fund A's already-inferior Sharpe therefore flatters it.

What the analysis cannot settle. 48 months is a short record, and with rare large losses driving Fund A's statistics, a handful of observations carry most of the estimates — all of these figures have wide standard errors and alpha in particular is unlikely to be statistically distinguishable from zero for either fund. The relationships are associational and backward-looking: correlation with the market of 0.7223 and 0.8338 describes the period observed, and correlations move in stressed markets, usually toward one, so the diversification implied by a correlation of 0.6156 between the funds may not be available when it is needed. There is nothing here on fees, liquidity, redemption terms, manager tenure, or strategy drift, any of which could change the conclusion. The recommendation is that B better fits the mandate on the evidence available, not that B is the better fund in all states of the world.
