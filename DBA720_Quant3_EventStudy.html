Event Study: Abnormal Returns and CAR

The identification strategy the course spends an entire meeting on — and the only named methodology reading.

Why this module exists. The market model you estimated in Assignment 1 — regressing returns on an index to get alpha and beta — is the estimation step of an event study. This module finishes the job. Expect the comprehensive examination to reach for this as a research-design prompt.

The setting

A publicly listed digital-asset custodian trades normally for 40 days. On day 0, before the open, a financial regulator announces an enforcement action against the firm. You have daily returns (%) for the company and for a broad market index.

Estimation window: days −42 through −3 (40 trading days). Event window: days −2 through +2 (5 days).

Excel work

  1. Using the estimation window only, regress firm return on market return. INTERCEPT gives α, SLOPE gives β.
  2. Get the standard deviation of the regression residuals with STEYX(firm, market) over the same window. This is your σ(AR).
  3. For each event day compute the expected return = α + β × Rmarket.
  4. AR = actual return − expected return, for each of the 5 days.
  5. CAR = sum of the 5 abnormal returns.
  6. Test statistic: t = CAR / ( σ(AR) × SQRT(5) ). Compare with the two-tailed 5% critical value of 1.96.
Watch the sign. A negative AR means the firm underperformed what its market exposure predicted. That is the direction an enforcement action should produce — if your figures come out positive, check that you subtracted expected from actual and not the reverse.

CAR = -4.6045%, t = -3.0366, which exceeds 1.96 in absolute value, so the null of no effect is rejected at the 5% level.

Notice the shape of the result. The abnormal return on the announcement day itself is -3.2777, and the days either side are much smaller and not all the same sign. Individual daily ARs are noisy; the CAR aggregates them, which is exactly why the method cumulates rather than testing a single day. Notice also that the pre-event days show little movement — weak evidence against a leak, though five days is far too short a window to establish that.

Interpretation

1. Running this on a cryptocurrency

Suppose the asset were Bitcoin rather than a listed company. Which parts of this design break, and what would you do instead?

Several parts break at once. First, the benchmark: the market model needs a market portfolio the asset is priced against, and there is no agreed market index for digital assets — using an equity index imports an exposure that may be near zero and regime-dependent, and using a crypto index makes the benchmark and the asset nearly the same thing. Second, the trading calendar: crypto trades continuously, so "day 0" is a convention rather than a fact, and news arriving on a weekend has no clean event date. Third, the distribution: t-statistics built on σ(AR) assume approximately normal residuals, and crypto returns are fat-tailed, so the test over-rejects — non-parametric tests such as a rank or sign test are safer. Fourth, anticipation: regulatory news in this sector leaks routinely, which widens the window you need and weakens identification as you widen it. Fifth — and this is the one candidates usually miss — the data itself. Le Pennec, Fiedler and Ante (2021) show that reported volumes at several exchanges are inflated by wash trading, so prices and volumes drawn from those venues may not reflect real trading at all. An event study is only as good as the price series underneath it.

2. Economic versus statistical significance

The CAR is statistically significant. What further question should a doctoral reader ask?

Whether it matters. A CAR of roughly 4.6% over five days is a real loss of value, but the significance test alone says only that it is unlikely to be noise, not that it is large relative to the firm, the industry, or the size of the penalty being announced. The reader should ask what the CAR implies in dollar terms against market capitalisation, how it compares with abnormal returns documented for similar enforcement events, and whether the magnitude is consistent with the economic mechanism claimed — a fine, a loss of customers, or a re-pricing of regulatory risk across the sector. A statistically significant result with an implausible magnitude usually signals a specification problem rather than a discovery.
